# Navigation in the Age of Sail (sample reference text)

Dead reckoning was the everyday method of estimating a ship's position. The navigator started from a known position and applied the ship's course, speed and the time elapsed. Errors accumulated quickly, so dead reckoning had to be corrected by observations of the sun or stars whenever the weather allowed.

Speed was measured with the chip log. A wooden board, the log-ship, was thrown over the stern on a line knotted at regular intervals. A sailor counted the knots that ran out while a sandglass of about twenty-eight seconds emptied. This is why a ship's speed is still given in knots.

The sextant is a navigation instrument used to measure the angle between a celestial body and the horizon. At local noon the navigator "shot the sun" with the sextant; the sun's altitude at that moment, corrected with tables in the nautical almanac, gave the ship's latitude.

Longitude was the harder problem. Because the Earth turns fifteen degrees every hour, longitude could be found by comparing local noon with the time at a reference meridian such as Greenwich. The marine chronometer, a spring-driven clock designed to keep accurate time despite the ship's motion and changes of temperature, made this comparison possible. John Harrison built the first successful marine chronometers in the eighteenth century.

The nautical almanac is a yearly book of tables giving the positions of the sun, moon, planets and navigational stars. Navigators used the almanac together with the sextant and the chronometer to reduce their sights to a position line.

The ship's day was divided into watches of four hours, marked by a bell struck every half hour. Eight bells signalled the end of a watch. The dog watches, two short watches of two hours each between four and eight in the evening, ensured that the crew did not stand the same watch every day.
